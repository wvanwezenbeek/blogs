*What are your long-term principles?* Now that I’m in my final year before turning 60, I thought it was a good time to learn from others, take on board new ideas, inspire one another and share that with everyone around us. With an emphasis on each other’s ‘long-term perspectives’. For me, these are transparency, reliability, resilience, accessibility and connection. I have reached out (and will continue to do so) to a few people I didn’t really know, who have piqued my curiosity, and with whom I’d like to have a conversation. The third connection I made was on 2 November 2026, with [Jessica Gardner](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dr-jessica-gardner-82719729/), University Librarian and Director of Library Services at the University of Cambridge. Marian Lefferts, Executive Manager of the Consortium of European Research Libraries (CERL) suggested I’d have a conversation with Jessica. <BR>

Jessica and I never met each other before, though our period as University Librarians has an overlap. Jessica started as University Librarian in Cambridge 9 years ago, and I said goodbye to TU Delft Library in 2020. We did share a colleague, [Marta Teperek](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mteperek/), who introduced the Data Champion programme in Cambridge, and continued her career in Delft (and now works for [Open Science NL](https://www.openscience.nl/en) (at NWO)). 

### Life long learning
One of the “long lines” for Jessica has been her work in and for libraries. During her study (she holds a doctorate in English Literature based on modern literary archives) at the University of Leeds she started working as a student in the library (“better than a student bar”), and via Exeter and Bristol she ended in Cambridge University Library, one of the legal deposit libraries of the United Kingdom and Ireland. With a humanities background she also could have chosen the researcher’s path. But “I am not a lone scholar”. Working within a team, for communities, and not alone, made her choose for (and stay with) libraries. 

We discussed a bit about the importance of **working together**. Even in research (perhaps more evolved in the context of a technical university where I studied and worked) we now talk about “team science”. The first team that is important to you is your own internal team, also when you work in senior management. Besides that there is the organisation, for Jessica of course the wider Cambridge university, where there are so many professional peers, but also academics, she engages with. The depth of the research culture and collections in Cambridge was a major attraction for her and is a connecting interest across her working life. And as (now past) chair or RLUK a rich network outside the university contributes to inspiration and learning.

From this topic we smoothly enter the subject of “life long learning”. Also for us, me being 59 and Jessica 55, that still counts. Perhaps we now know better what choices we have made, what stayed with us, what matured, and what changed. Our age makes us wonder “what is the good we can do” – and how we can be true to ourselves. We are not so focused on “powerplay”, particularly as we have both already achieved significant career goals, though we still need to show courage, resilience and leadership, but that feels more comfortable than twenty years ago.

### "It is all connected"
When Jessica applied for the position in Cambridge, she could very well explain what she would bring. “You choose an organisation the same way as it choses you, but you want them to choose you for what you bring and what you want to do”. And how very nice that Jessica brought **“open”** to the university library. “Open as a practice, as a philosophy, in your building, collections, and services, and how you behave”. It is all connected, and one of the reasons she has always worked in a public organisation: “Participation is so important”. Of course you need to realize that Cambridge University is a very ancient organisation, where women were finally allowed to be awarded a degree in 1948, not that long ago. The pace of the organisation is a slow one. But “the scale for what we are here for brings a responsibility and a rare expectation to think and act for the long-term”, and that is a perfect match with her motivation and practice. 

I guess that Jessica and I could have talked for hours (which we did not, we had a very nice chat via Teams for 45 minutes)! We both feel very engaged, where we understand that “the information we hold of the past, has value for the future”. **Stewardship** is the heart of the profession. And the digital world gives us opportunities of sharing that were not possible before. Institutes of authority, trust and evident – it is at one hand a huge responsibility and at the other hand we are not alone (and we cannot solve our big challenges alone). In the UK there is the [Libraries Alliance](https://librariesalliance.org.uk/), where a bridge is created across all libraries (school, prison, university, heritage, public and national). For some of the work we do we serve the same communities, and have the same conversations. It might “play out in different ways”, but everywhere you find the question of freedom of thought, access and censorship. “Libraries are the very few places you do not need to pay to get into”. A place of belonging for the many and a place embodying the freedom to read.

### Slow thinking
Having acknowledged that it is not always easy to keep standing strong, we talk about skills and leadership that is needed for the world we live in today. 
You do need sources of inspiration to keep motivated. And for Jessica there is no singular answer to where her inspiration lies. It can be in a conversation with a student who is telling a powerful story. For her the way to get to “slow thinking” is reading, but not to cover enormous amounts of digital bites. For her “the yoga of the mind” is reading a poem. She also loves the answer she heard coming from a senior librarian early in her career. He was asked what the most important work in his library was: “It is that one there, being put down on the table, because that book is connected to someone who needs it”. The value lies in how your collection is used. And this example, and inspiration, shows that you should make yourself a bit less important. It is about the people and the users, and the library as a bridge connecting communities and information, physical and digital.
