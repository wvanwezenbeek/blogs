*What are your long-term principles?* Now that I’m in my final year before turning 60, I thought it was a good time to learn from others, take on board new ideas, inspire one another and share that with everyone around us. With an emphasis on each other’s ‘long-term perspectives’. For me, these are transparency, reliability, resilience, accessibility and connection. I have reached out (and will continue to do so) to a few people I didn’t really know, who have piqued my curiosity, and with whom I’d like to have a conversation. The second connection I made was on 9 September 2026, with [Nicole Teeuwen](https://www.nicoleteeuwen.nl/), chair of the Practical Training Sector Council.

I came across Nicole Teeuwen through my colleague Elsbeth Kwant. Nicole is chair of the Practical Training Sector Council and invited me – on a rainy afternoon, though the sun did peek through now and then – to GSG Het Segment, a school for practical training in Gouda, The Netherlands. We met on a Wednesday afternoon, and after our conversation we were given a private tour of the school by a second-year pupil. Our conversation began with the here and now. It was the day after [the PISA results (Dutch page)](https://www.pisa-nederland.nl/resultaten/) were announced. And amidst all the negative talk about the standard of our reading skills, the fact that the reading skills of pupils in practical training have improved in recent years may have been somewhat overlooked. Pupils at practical training schools scored an average of 7 points higher than four years ago. And that is certainly significant!

### Moving forward
Throughout her long and rewarding career, Nicole has dedicated herself to giving people a helping hand – through policy and creating the right conditions, because that is what she does best. In this way, she helps people move forward by making the most of their talents. She also gives children in vocational education good prospects for the future. She herself was given the opportunity to study. Her parents had only attended primary school. And she grew up in a neighbourhood in Utrecht (‘wijk C’) where her grandfather also worked alongside the day labourers. They lugged market stalls about, peeled prawns – whatever needed doing and came their way. And in a way, that’s (albeit adapted to the present day) what the children in practical training will end up doing too. The link between her background and what she stands for is very clear.

### Stay connected
Nicole explains that she has become socially mobile; and that this has also created a distance between her and her family – they no longer understood one another. How wonderful, then, that she has managed to forge that connection after all. She is now doing so in the role she has held for the longest time (certainly once her terms as chairperson have come to an end). But she has also been able to help people get ahead during her time working for the trade union (Abvakabo), in care for people with disabilities, and as director of the telephone helpline. A very clear, long-standing trajectory and driving force. She has broadened this by being active in local politics in Houten, and by still sitting on various supervisory boards.

### Reading while doing
As we walk around the school, we see the practical training rooms, where you can learn to weld, saw, do carpentry, repair a car, bicycle or scooter, cook, and look after animals. And much more besides. It’s lovely to see everyone so committed; every subject teacher proudly explains what they are doing with the pupils.
And Nicole would like to add one more thing. To come back to the improved reading skills: “Learning doesn’t have to come solely from a textbook. Pupils also learn to read whilst cooking. And it works. Stop forcing everyone into the same cognitive mould.” And that touches on something Nicole considers truly vital. Give vocational education its own place; not at the bottom of the ladder, but alongside academic education. So that we can value diverse and different qualities side by side. And so that we don’t create marginalized groups of people whom we desperately need in society. Nicole might well want to capture that passion and insight in a book one day. And that strikes me as a very good idea. Because, through her drive, Nicole makes society accessible to everyone. She also draws inspiration for this from her colleague (Chair of the Education Council) Louise Elffers, who also attaches great importance to equality of opportunity.

### Stop emphasizing the difference 
Nicole believes that Arie Slob’s decision (then Minister for Education) during the pandemic to advise on ‘opportunity-based’ education may have seemed positive at first glance, but that this subsequently led to a great deal of disappointment and demotivation. You end up walking on tiptoes at the start, only to fall much further below your actual level once you become disillusioned. According to Nicole, this is why vocational education has grown relatively rapidly. It reminds me of my aversion to the pressure to excel in the university environments where I’ve worked. After all, you always measure excellence against others; just give it the best you have – that seems more than enough to me. We carry on talking for a while about how to ensure that the pupils at this school will also feel valued in the work they do later on. ‘Take people seriously,’ says Nicole, ‘that’s incredibly important. And stop emphasizing the differences so much.’ Ultimately, she’s firmly convinced that people like us won’t be needed any more (new technology will take our place; it can also be used for decision-making), but handcraft and service provision will endure.

### A full circle 
Nicole now lives in Utrecht again, and when she attends a meeting of one of her supervisory boards, she looks out directly onto the very place where she used to live. She’s come full circle. But then, you only realize that in hindsight.
